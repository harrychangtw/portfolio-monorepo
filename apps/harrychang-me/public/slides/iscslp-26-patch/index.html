Hi everyone, I'm Richard Tsai from Academia Sinica and National Central University. Today I'll talk about PATCH, a safety dataset for Traditional Chinese.

Let me start with why we did this. A lot of people now run small LLMs because they're cheap and can run locally. But small models are hard to align well on their own, so the usual approach is to put a safety classifier in front and filter harmful inputs. Llama Guard 3 1B is a common choice. The problem is it struggles with Traditional Chinese. Each dot here is a harmful prompt written by a person. The hollow ones are the ones it missed, about 38 percent. So roughly one in three gets through.

Why does this happen? We see three reasons. First, there's just less data. Traditional Chinese has millions of users in Taiwan, Hong Kong and overseas, but there's much less safety data than for Simplified Chinese. Second, translating English data isn't enough. Translation strips out the local parts: regional slang, cultural context, and things like Zhuyin spelling. Third, most Chinese safety benchmarks are in Simplified Chinese, and many are multiple choice or score model responses. They're not really built for training a classifier on the input side.

So we built PATCH. There are about 825,000 synthetic prompts, 593,000 safe and 232,000 unsafe. The unsafe ones follow the 13 MLCommons hazard categories, the same taxonomy Llama Guard 3 uses, so results compare directly. We also have 390 prompts written from scratch by people. We call that PATCH-H. It's never used for training, only for testing, and the three annotators agreed well, with a Fleiss' kappa of 0.84. As a side note, we also ran PATCH-H against a range of large models. Attack success rates went from 13 to 98 percent, so even recent models get fooled.

So how did we build it? There are four tracks. (click) The first is safe data. We took Simplified Chinese prompts from ChatGPT-Corpus, converted them with a tool called Fanhuaji, which also swaps in Taiwanese wording, and then had gpt-4o-mini paraphrase and lengthen them. We lengthened them because harmful prompts tend to be longer, and we didn't want the classifier to just learn length. (click) The second is PATCH-GPT. We simply asked GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 to write harmful prompts for each of the 13 categories. These are fairly direct. (click) The third, PATCH-RT, is the interesting part. It follows the idea of Rainbow Teaming. A 32B Mutator comes up with new attacks, a 14B Judge decides whether they work, and the ones that work go back to the Mutator for more variations. At the end, a small 3B model adds Traditional Chinese specific phrasing, like Zhuyin or local slang. (click) The fourth is PATCH-H. Three native-speaker NLP researchers wrote these prompts without ever seeing the synthetic data, then reviewed each other's work. (click) After automated filtering and manual spot checks, the first three tracks make up PATCH itself. PATCH-H is kept separate, just for testing.

Here are a few real examples from PATCH-RT. (click) The first one fakes authority. It makes up a "Civil Self-Defense Ordinance" and cites the Ministry of the Interior to make violence sound legal. (click) The second wraps the request in local context. It talks about the old Taiwanese mafia and quotes a Taiwanese saying, "do it quietly," so the crime reads like a history lesson. (click) The third changes the spelling. "Sexual harassment" is typed out in Zhuyin, so a keyword filter never sees it. None of these would show up if you just translated English data.

For the experiments we used three models. Llama Guard 3 1B is the main baseline, since it was built for safety classification. The other two, RoBERTa and Longformer, are encoder models. We compared three ways to adapt them: full fine-tuning, LoRA, and Chat-Vector. LoRA trains less than one percent of the parameters. Chat-Vector needs no training at all; it adds a safety parameter difference and a Traditional Chinese one together. It didn't work well, and the details are in the backup slides. We evaluate on three things, numbered here one to three, and the results table follows the same order.

This table shows F1 on all three evaluations. The top two rows are Llama Guard as it comes. (click) First column, the PATCH test split. It starts at 0.781, and with LoRA it reaches 0.996. That's basically the same as full fine-tuning at 0.997, while training under one percent of the parameters. All three models end up above 0.98. (click) But doing well on synthetic data doesn't prove much, so the second column is PATCH-H, written by people. It goes from 0.754 to 0.869 with LoRA. Recall goes from 0.623 to 0.946, so most of that 38 percent we missed at the start is now caught. Interestingly, full fine-tuning only gets 0.711 here, which is worse than doing nothing. (click) The last column is English. We only trained on Traditional Chinese, but English got better too, from 0.773 to 0.950. Our guess is that LoRA changes so few parameters that the model keeps its English ability, while it picks up tricks like role-play and obfuscation that don't depend on language. Full fine-tuning does more damage. Longformer drops to 0.046 on English.

To wrap up: PATCH is the first large-scale adversarial safety dataset for Traditional Chinese. Training with LoRA on PATCH is cheap and gives a classifier that holds up against prompts written by people. And what it learns in Traditional Chinese carries over to English. If someone asks about Chat-Vector: it adds Llama Guard's safety vector and a Traditional Chinese language vector in some ratio. The more weight on safety, the higher the precision, but recall keeps dropping. The best mix only reaches an F1 of 0.676, which is below the untouched model at 0.781. So our takeaway is that you can't get safety just by adding parameters together. You still need to train on data.

If someone asks how attack success was measured: three models, GPT-4o, Gemini 2.5 Pro and DeepSeek V3, vote on whether each response is harmful. To check that this is reliable, we compared it with human labels on 4,416 responses. Cohen's kappa was 0.75 with 88 percent accuracy, and the judges lean conservative, so they'd rather call something harmful than miss it. On data quality, about a quarter of the unsafe synthetic prompts were checked by hand, and problems we found fed back into the generation prompts.
